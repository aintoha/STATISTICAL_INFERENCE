1.1.1 Uniform (discrete) Distribution

Definition 1.1 (Uniform Distribution) A random variable \(X\) follows the discrete uniform distribution on the interval \([a, a+1, \dots, b]\), if it may attain each of these values with equal probability

\[f(x) = \frac{1}{N}$ for $x=1,2,..,N\]

where \(N\) is the parameterss

1.1.1.1 Properties of Uniform Distribution

Note: You may need to show how to obtain the mean and variance. Refer to the previous chapter how to derive the mean and variance of discrete distribution.

  • Mean: \(\frac{N+1}{2}\)

  • Variance: \(\frac{N^2-1}{12}\)

  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \sum_{x=1}^{N} e^{tx} \frac{1}{N}\)

Example 1.1 Let \(x\) equal the number of spots on the side facing upward after a six-sided die is cast at random. If everything is fair about this experiment, a reasonable probability model is given by the p.m.f

Find the mean and variance of the random variable \(X\).

** Solution**

Since \(X\) follow the discrete uniform distribution with parameter \(N=6\), then;

\[\begin{align*} E[X] =& \frac{N + 1}{2} = \frac{6 + 1}{2} = 3.5 \\ Var[X] =& \frac{N^2 - 1}{12} = \frac{6^2 - 1}{12} = \frac{35}{12} \end{align*}\]

1.1.1.2 CDF Uniform Uniform Distribution

Theorem: A random variable \(X\) follows the discrete uniform distribution on the interval \([a,a+1,…,b]\), if it may attain each of these values with equal probability. We have then ;

\[\begin{align*} P(X=x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{n} & \text{for } a \le x \le b \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{align*}\]

\[\begin{align*} F(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{for } x < a \\ \frac{x-a+1}{n} & \text{for } a \le x \le b \\ 1 & \text{for } x > b \end{cases} \end{align*}\]

Where \(n=b-a+1\) denotes the number of values that \(X\) may take

1.1.2 Bernoulli Distribution

A Bernoulli experiment is a random experiment with two mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes, for instance, success (1) and failure (0).

  • PMF: \(f(x,p) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}\) for \(x \in \{0, 1\}\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = p\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = pq\) (where \(q = 1-p\))
  • **MGF^{*}**: \(M_X(t) = pe^t + q\)

1.1.3 Binomial Distribution

A sequence of \(n\) Bernoulli trials such that when a Bernoulli experiment is performed several independent times and the probability of success—say, \(p\)—remains the same from trial to trial.

The number of ways of selecting the \(r\) positions for the \(x\) successes in \(n\) trials is

\[\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}\] Since the probabilities of success and failure on each trial are \(p\) and \((1-p)\) respectively, the probability of each of these ways is

\[p^{x}(1 - p)^{1 = x}\] Then, for \(f(x) \ge 0\),

\[\sum f_X(x) = \sum_{x=0}^{n} \binom{n}{x} p^x (q)^{n-x} = ((1-p) + p)^n = 1\]

Definition 1.2 (Binomial Distribution) A random variable \(X\) is defined to have a binomial distribution if the pmf of \(X\) is given by

f(x; n, p) = \[\begin{cases} \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} & \text{for } x = 0, 1, \dots, n \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

Proof (Binomial Distribution Properties). Let \(X\) be a random variable with binomial distribution that has parameter \(p\) and \(n\). Derive the following:

  1. \(E[X] = np\)

  2. \(Var[X] = np(1 - p)\)

Proof

  1. Deriving the means

\[E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \cdot P(X=x)\]

Substitute the probability mass function (PMF) of the binomial distribution, \(P(X=x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\):

\[E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\]

Expand the binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}\):\[E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\]

Since the first term of the sum (when \(x=0\)) is \(0\), we can start the summation at \(x=1\):\[E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^{n} x \frac{n!}{x(x-1)!(n-x)!} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\]

Cancel the \(x\) in the numerator and denominator:

\[E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^{n} \frac{n!}{(x-1)!(n-x)!} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\]

To reshape this into another binomial expansion, factor out one \(n\) and one \(p\):

\[E[X] = np \sum_{x=1}^{n} \frac{(n-1)!}{(x-1)!(n-x)!} p^{x-1} (1-p)^{n-x}\]

Now, use a change of variables. Let \(y = x - 1\).As \(x\) goes from \(1\) to \(n\), \(y\) goes from \(0\) to \(n-1\).The exponent \((n-x)\) becomes \((n - 1 - y)\).Substitute \(y\) into the summation:

\[E[X] = np \sum_{y=0}^{n-1} \frac{(n-1)!}{y!(n-1-y)!} p^y (1-p)^{n-1-y}\]

Rewrite the fractional term as a binomial coefficient:\[E[X] = np \sum_{y=0}^{n-1} \binom{n-1}{y} p^y (1-p)^{n-1-y}\]

According to the Binomial Theorem, \(\sum_{k=0}^{m} \binom{m}{k} a^k b^{m-k} = (a+b)^m\).

Applying this to our sum where \(m = n-1\), \(a = p\), and \(b = 1-p\):

\[E[X] = np (p + (1-p))^{n-1}\]

where

1.1.4 Geometric Distribution

Let \(X\) equal the number of trials until the first success is obtained in a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials. The event \(X = x\) corresponds to \(x – 1\) failures, and then a success. Thus,

\[P(X=x) = (1-p)^{x-1}p\]

for \(x=1, 2, 3, \dots\)

1.1.4.1 Derivation of Geometric Distribution CDF

The cumulative distribution function (CDF), \(F(x)\), represents the probability that the first success occurs on or before the \(x^\text{th}\) trial. It is derived by summing the probability mass function from \(k = 1\) to \(x\):

\[F(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{x} (1 - p)^{k-1}p\]

Factor out the constant probability of success, \(p\):

\[F(x) = p \sum_{k=1}^{x} (1 - p)^{k-1}\]

The summation is a finite geometric series with a common ratio of \((1 - p)\). Using the standard formula for the sum of a finite geometric series, \(\sum_{k=1}^{x} r^{k-1} = \frac{1 - r^x}{1 - r}\) where \(r = (1-p)\):

\[F(x) = p \left( \frac{1 - (1 - p)^x}{1 - (1 - p)} \right)\]

Simplify the denominator, noting that \(1 - (1 - p) = p\):

\[F(x) = \frac{p(1 - (1 - p)^x)}{p}\]

Cancel the \(p\) in the numerator and denominator to arrive at the final CDF:

\[F(x) = 1 - (1 - p)^x\]

1.1.4.2 Properties of Geometric Distribution

  • Mean: \(\mu = \frac{1}{p}\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{q}{p^2}\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \frac{pe^t}{1 - qe^t}\)

1.1.4.3 Geometric Distribution: Examples

  1. A die is tossed repeatedly. What is the probability that we observe a 3 for the first time on the \(8^\text{th}\) toss?

  2. A basketball player has a 75% chance of making a free throw. What is the probability that the player makes their first free throw on the 3rd attempt?

  3. A manufacturing machine produces items with a 5% defect rate. An inspector checks items one by one. What is the probability that the first defective item found is the 10th item inspected?

  4. A telemarketer has a 10% chance of making a sale on any given call. What is the probability that they make their first sale on their 6th call of the day?

Example 1.2 A die was tossed repeatedly. What is the probability that we observe a 3 for the first time on the 8th toss?

Solution

This random variable is having a geometric distribution since we wanted to find what is the probability of getting success(3) on the 8th trial. So here, the parameter for this distribution is \(p = \frac{1}{6}\) since the probability of getting 3 in each trial is \(\frac{1}{6}\).

Hence the pmf of \(X\) is \[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= (1 - p)^{x-1}(p) \\ &= \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{x-1}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right) \end{aligned} \]

Since we wanted to find the probability when X=8,

\[ \begin{aligned} P(X = 8) &= f(8) \\ &= \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{8-1}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right) \approx 0.0465 \end{aligned} \]

Example 1.3 A basketball player has a 75% chance of making a free throw. What is the probability that the player makes their first free throw on the 3rd attempt?

Solution:

This follows a geometric distribution because we are looking for the first success (making the shot) on a specific trial.The probability of success on a single attempt is \(p = 0.75\), and the probability of failure is \(1 - p = 0.25\).To find the probability of the first success on the 3rd attempt (\(X = 3\)), apply the geometric PMF:

\[P(X = 3) = (0.25)^{3-1}(0.75) = (0.25)^2(0.75) = 0.046875\]

Example 1.4 A manufacturing machine produces items with a 5% defect rate. An inspector checks items one by one. What is the probability that the first defective item found is the 10th item inspected?

Solution:

Here, a “success” is defined as finding a defective item.The probability of success is \(p = 0.05\), and the probability of failure (a functional item) is \(1 - p = 0.95\).We want the probability of the first success on the 10th trial (\(X = 10\)):

\[P(X = 10) = (0.95)^{10-1}(0.05) = (0.95)^9(0.05) \approx 0.0315\]

Exercise 1.3 A telemarketer has a 10% chance of making a sale on any given call. What is the probability that they make their first sale on their 6th call of the day?

Solution:

The probability of making a sale (success) is \(p = 0.10\), and the probability of not making a sale is \(1 - p = 0.90\).We want the first success to occur on the 6th call (\(X = 6\)):\[P(X = 6) = (0.90)^{6-1}(0.10) = (0.90)^5(0.10) \approx 0.0590\]

Exercise 1.4 Suppose the telemarketer wants to be at least 80% sure that they will make at least one sale today. How many calls (\(n\)) do they need to make to achieve this probability?

Solution:

Using the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) for the geometric distribution, the probability of getting the first success on or before the \(n^\text{th}\) trial is \(P(X \le n) = 1 - (1 - p)^n\).

Set up the inequality with the target probability of 0.80 and \(p = 0.10\): \[1 - (1 - 0.10)^n > 0.80\] \[1 - (0.90)^n > 0.80\]

Isolate the term with \(n\): \[1 - 0.80 > (0.90)^n\] \[0.20 > (0.90)^n\]

Take the natural logarithm (\(\ln\)) of both sides to bring down the exponent: \[\ln(0.20) > \ln((0.90)^n)\] \[\ln(0.20) > n \ln(0.90)\]

Divide by \(\ln(0.90)\). Because \(\ln(0.90)\) is a negative number, the inequality sign flips: \[n > \frac{\ln(0.20)}{\ln(0.90)}\]

Calculate the numerical value: \[n > \frac{-1.6094}{-0.1054}\] \[n > 15.27\]

Since the telemarketer cannot make a fraction of a call, we round up to the next whole integer.

The telemarketer must make 16 calls to have a greater than 80% probability of making their first sale.

1.1.5 Negative Binomial Distribution

  • In the Negative Binomial distribution, we want to find the number of trials needed to achieve the \(r^{\text{th}}\) success.

  • Let \(X\) be the random variable representing the total number of trials required until the \(r^{\text{th}}\) success is obtained.

  • To understand how the probability mass function (PMF) is constructed, we break the sequence of trials into two distinct parts:

Part 1: The first \(x-1\) trials

For the \(r^{\text{th}}\) success to occur exactly on the \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial, there must be exactly \(r-1\) successes occurring somewhere within the first \(x-1\) trials.

Because the order of those earlier successes and failures doesn’t matter, the number of ways to arrange them is given by the binomial coefficient \(\binom{x-1}{r-1}\). Using standard binomial probability logic, the probability of having exactly \(r-1\) successes and \(x-r\) failures by time \(x-1\) is:

\[ \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^{r-1} q^{(x-1)-(r-1)} = \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^{r-1} q^{x-r} \]

(Where \(p\) is the constant probability of success, and \(q = 1-p\) is the probability of failure).

Part 2: The \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial

Since the \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial must be the final success that stops the sequence, its probability is simply \(p\). Using the multiplicative rule for independent events, we multiply the probability from Part 1 by \(p\) to get the final PMF of \(X=x\):

\[ p \cdot \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^{r-1} q^{x-r} = \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^r q^{x-r} \]

Definition 1.4 Let \(X\) be the random variable with the PMF

\[P(X = x) = \binom{x - 1}{r - 1} p^r (1 - p)^{x-r}\]

where \(x = r, r + 1,\)

Then it is a probability distribution called as negative binomial distribution with parameter \(r\) and \(p\).

Remarks, if $ r=1$, then the negative binomial distribution was just basically identical to geometric distribution.

1.1.5.1 Summary of Parameters:

  • \(x\): Total number of trials (\(x = r, r+1, r+2, \dots\))
  • \(r\): Target number of successes
  • \(p\): Probability of success on any given independent trial
  • \(q\): Probability of failure (\(1 - p\))

Note: The Geometric distribution is a special case of the Negative Binomial distribution where we are looking for the very first success (\(r=1\)).

1.1.5.2 Properties

  • PMF: \(P(X=x) = \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^r (1-p)^{x-r}\) for \(x=r, r+1, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = \frac{r}{p}\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{rq}{p^2}\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \left( \frac{pe^t}{1 - qe^t} \right)^r\)

Example 1.5 Team A plays team B in a seven-game world series. That is, the series is over when either team wins four games. For each game, \(P(A \ wins) = 0.6\) and the games are assumed independent. What is the probability that the series will end in exactly six games? We have \(x = 6, r = 4 ,\) and \(p = 0.6\).

Case 1: A win four game

Since A winning those four game, we P(success)=P (A wins)=0.6. This can be a probability of Negative binomial with parameter r=4 and p=0.6 when \(X=6\).

Hence

\[\begin{align*} P(\text{A win series in 6}) &= NegBin(6,4,0.6) \\ &= \binom{6-1}{4-1} (0.6)^4(0.4)^{6-4} \\ &= 0.20736 \end{align*}\]

Case 2: B win Four game Since \(B\) winning those four game,

\[P(success)=P(B wins)=0.4\]

This can be a probability of Negative binomial with parameter \(r=4\) and p=0.4 when X=6.

Hence

\[\begin{align*} P(\text{B win series in 6}) &= NegBin(6,4,0.4) \\ &= \binom{6-1}{4-1} (0.4)^4(0.6)^{6-4} \\ &= 0.09216 \end{align*}\]

We wanted to find the probability the series end in \(6\) game. So it can be either A win series in \(6\) game or B. since the event is mutually exclusive, than

\[\begin{align*} P(\text{series end in 6 game}) &= P(\text{A win series in 6}) + P(\text{B win series in 6}) \\ &= 0.20736 + 0.09216 \\ &= 0.29952 \end{align*}\]

Exercise 1.5 An excellent free-throw shooter attempts several free throws until she misses.

  1. If \(p = 0.9\) is her probability of making a free throw, what is the probability of having the first miss on the 13th attempt or later?

  2. If she continues shooting until she misses three, what is the probability that the third miss occurs on the 30th attempt?

Solution

  1. For this problem, because the sequence stops upon a “miss”, we must define a “success” for our formulas as missing the free throw.

  • Probability of making a throw: \(P(make) = 0.9\)

  • Probability of missing (our “success” event): \(p_{miss} = 1 - 0.9 = 0.1\)

To have the first miss on the 13th attempt or later, the shooter must successfully make all of the first 12 attempts. Since each throw is independent, we simply multiply the probability of making a throw 12 times:

\[\begin{align*} P(\text{first miss} \ge 13) &= P(\text{makes first 12 throws}) \\ &= (0.9)^{12} \\ &\approx 0.2824 \end{align*}\]

  1. This scenario follows a Negative Binomial distribution where we want the \(r^{\text{th}}\) “success” (miss) to occur on the \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial.

    • \(x = 30\) (total trials)

    • \(r = 3\) (target number of misses)

    • \(p = 0.1\) (probability of a miss)

Using the Negative Binomial probability mass function:

\[P(X = x) = \binom{x - 1}{r - 1} p^r (1 - p)^{x-r}\]

Substitute the values:

\[\begin{align*} P(X = 30) &= \binom{30 - 1}{3 - 1} (0.1)^3 (0.9)^{30-3} \\ &= \binom{29}{2} (0.1)^3 (0.9)^{27} \\ &= \frac{29 \times 28}{2} (0.001) (0.9)^{27} \\ &= 406 \times 0.001 \times 0.0581497 \\ &\approx 0.0236 \end{align*}\]

1.1.6 Hypergeometric Distribution

Used for sampling \(n\) individuals without replacement from a finite population of size \(N\) containing \(M\) successes.

\(P(X=x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x}\binom{N-M}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}}\) for \(\max(0, n - N + M) \le x \le \min(n, M)\)

The assumptions leading to the hypergeometric distribution are as follows:

  1. The population or set to be sampled consists of \(N\) individuals, objects, or elements (a finite population).

  2. Each individual can be characterized as a success (\(S\)) or a failure (\(F\)), and there are \(M\) successes in the population.

  3. A sample of \(n\) individuals is selected without replacement in such a way that each subset of size \(n\) is equally likely to be chosen.

By definition; let n be the number of samples drawn from the population without replacement and let random variable X be the number of green ball drawn. Its pmf is given by the hypergeometric distribution

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x}\binom{N-M}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}} \]

for \(x\), an integer, satisfying max \((0, n - N + M) \le x \le \text{min } (n, M)\).

1.1.6.1 Properties

If \(X\) is a random variable with hypergeometric distribution with parameter \(N\), \(M\) and \(n\). then

  1. \(\text{Mean} = n \cdot \frac{M}{N}\)

  2. \(\text{Variance} = \left(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\right) \cdot n \cdot \frac{M}{n} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{M}{N}\right)\)

(#exm:hypergeometric_distr-example1) A lot consisting of \(50\) bulbs is inspected by taking at random \(10\) bulbs and testing them.If the number of defective bulbs is at most \(1\), the lot is accepted; otherwise it is rejected. If there are in fact, \(10\) defective bulbs in the lot, what is the probability that the lot would be accepted?

Solution: We use the hypergeometric distribution because we are sampling without replacement from a finite population.

  • \(N = 50\) (total bulbs)

  • \(M = 10\) (total defective bulbs in the population)

  • \(n = 10\) (number of bulbs sampled)

  • \(x\) = number of defective bulbs in the sample

The lot is accepted if the number of defective bulbs is at most 1 (\(x \le 1\)). So we need to find \(P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)\).

\[\begin{align} P(X \le 1) &= P(X=0) + P(X=1) \\ &= \frac{\binom{10}{0}\binom{50-10}{10-0}}{\binom{50}{10}} + \frac{\binom{10}{1}\binom{50-10}{10-1}}{\binom{50}{10}} \\ &= \frac{\binom{10}{0}\binom{40}{10}}{\binom{50}{10}} + \frac{\binom{10}{1}\binom{40}{9}}{\binom{50}{10}} \\ &\approx 0.0825 + 0.2667 \\ &\approx 0.3492 \end{align}\]

(#exm:hypergeometric_distr-example2) If \(5\) cards are drawn at random from a deck of \(52\) cards and are not replaced, find the probability of getting at least one diamond.

Solution: We use the hypergeometric distribution because we are drawing without replacement. * \(N = 52\) (total cards) * \(M = 13\) (total diamonds in a deck) * \(n = 5\) (cards drawn) * \(x\) = number of diamonds drawn

We want to find the probability of getting at least one diamond, which is \(P(X \ge 1)\). The easiest way to calculate this is using the complement rule: \(1 - P(X = 0)\).

\[\begin{align} P(X \ge 1) &= 1 - P(X = 0) \\ &= 1 - \frac{\binom{13}{0}\binom{52-13}{5-0}}{\binom{52}{5}} \\ &= 1 - \frac{\binom{13}{0}\binom{39}{5}}{\binom{52}{5}} \\ &= 1 - \frac{1 \times 575757}{2598960} \\ &\approx 1 - 0.2215 \\ &\approx 0.7785 \end{align}\]

1.1.7 Poisson Distribution

Let the number of occurrences of some event in a given continuous interval be counted. Then we have an approximate Poisson process with parameter \(\lambda > 0\) if the following conditions are satisfied:

  1. The numbers of occurrences in nonoverlapping subintervals are independent.

  2. The probability of exactly one occurrence in a sufficiently short subinterval of length \(h\) is approximately \(\lambda h\).

  3. The probability of two or more occurrences in a sufficiently short subinterval is essentially zero.

Suppose that we wanted to find the probability of number of occurrence, \(x\) of event in a time period given the length of the period and the average events per time. than the pmf of X is

\[P(x \text{ events in time period}) = e^{\left(\frac{-\text{events}}{\text{time}}\right)(\text{time period})} * \frac{\left(\left(\frac{\text{events}}{\text{time}}\right)(\text{time period})\right)^x}{x!}\]

Usually, in describing poisson distribution, we mainly concerning on the rate of occurrence which events/time * time period is usually simplified into a single parameter, \(\lambda\), lambda, the rate parameter.

Hence, Deff: a random variable X is said to have poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda > 0\) if its pmf is given by

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} \] Where \(x = 0,1,2,3, \ldots\)

To prove that the Poisson distribution is a valid probability distribution, we need to show that

\[\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} = 1\]

Proof From maclaurin series, it is given that

\[e^\lambda = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!}\]

So we can rewrite the pmf of X to be \[ \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} = e^{-\lambda} \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!} = e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda} = 1 \]

Hence it is proven.

If \(X\) is a random variable of poisson distribution wth parameter \(\lambda>0\), then

  1. Mean \(= \lambda\)

  2. Variance \(= \lambda\)

  3. \(M_X(t) = e^{\lambda(e^t - 1)}\)

Describes the number of occurrences of an event in a given continuous interval with an average rate \(\lambda > 0\).

  • PMF: \(P(X=x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}\) for \(x = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = \lambda\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \lambda\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}\)

1.2 Continuous Distributions

As in the case of discrete variables, continuous random variables may be described by known probability density functions (PDFs).

1.2.1 Continuous Uniform Distribution \(U(a,b)\)

A continuous random variable \(X\) has a Uniform distribution if the probability of any possible value occurring is the same over the interval \([a, b]\).

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}\) for \(a \le x \le b\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = \frac{a+b}{2}\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}\)

1.2.2 Exponential Distribution \(Exp(\theta)\)

Normally associated with “waiting-time” between events. It is memoryless.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\theta} e^{-x/\theta}\) for \(x > 0\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = \theta\) (Note: \(\theta = 1/\lambda\))
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \theta^2\)

1.2.3 Gamma Distribution \(Gamma(\alpha, \theta)\)

Defined as the waiting time for the \(\alpha\)-th occurrence. It describes phenomena that are skewed to the right.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^\alpha} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-x/\theta}\) for \(x \ge 0\)
  • Parameters: \(\theta\) (scale) and \(\alpha\) (shape).
  • Mean: \(\mu = \alpha\theta\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \alpha\theta^2\)

1.2.4 Chi-Squared Distribution \(\chi^2(r)\)

A special case of a Gamma distribution where \(\alpha = r/2\) and \(\theta = 2\). It is mainly used in statistical significance tests (like goodness of fit).

  • Parameter: \(r\), a positive integer specifying the degrees of freedom.
  • Mean: \(\mu = r\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = 2r\)

1.2.4.1 Normal Distribution \(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\)

The most widely used model, giving a symmetric bell-shaped curve.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\) for \(-\infty \le x \le \infty\)
  • Mean: \(\mu\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = e^{\mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2}}\)

1.2.5 Standard Normal Distribution

To compute probabilities for the normal distribution, we standardize \(X\) to \(Z\): \[Z = \frac{X-\mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0,1)\]

To find the probability \(P(a \le X \le b)\): \[P(a \le X \le b) = P\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma} \le Z \le \frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\]
