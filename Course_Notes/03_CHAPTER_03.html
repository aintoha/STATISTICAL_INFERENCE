Uniform (discrete) Distribution

A random variable $X$ follows the discrete uniform distribution on the interval $[a, a+1, \dots, b]$, if it may attain each of these values with equal probability

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{N}$ for $x=1,2,..,N$$

where $N$ is the parameterss

Properties of Uniform Distribution

  • Variance: \(\frac{N^2-1}{12}\)

  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \sum_{x=1}^{N} e^{tx} \frac{1}{N}\)

Bernoulli Distribution

A Bernoulli experiment is a random experiment with two mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes, for instance, success (1) and failure (0).

  • PMF: \(f(x,p) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}\) for \(x \in \{0, 1\}\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = p\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = pq\) (where \(q = 1-p\))
  • **MGF^{*}**: \(M_X(t) = pe^t + q\)

Binomial Distribution

A sequence of \(n\) Bernoulli trials such that when a Bernoulli experiment is performed several independent times and the probability of success—say, \(p\)—remains the same from trial to trial.

The number of ways of selecting the \(r\) positions for the \(x\) successes in \(n\) trials is

\[\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}\] Since the probabilities of success and failure on each trial are \(p\) and \((1-p)\) respectively, the probability of each of these ways is

\[p^{x}(1 - p)^{1 = x}\] Then, for \(f(x) \ge 0\),

\[\sum f_X(x) = \sum_{x=0}^{n} \binom{n}{x} p^x (q)^{n-x} = ((1-p) + p)^n = 1\]

A random variable $X$ is defined to have a binomial distribution if the pmf of $X$ is given by

f(x; n, p) = \begin{cases} 
\binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x} & \text{for } x = 0, 1, \dots, n \\ 
0 & \text{otherwise} 
\end{cases}

Let $X$ be a random variable with binomial distribution that has parameter $p$ and $n$. **Derive** the following: 

1. $E[X] = np$

2. $Var[X] = np(1 -  p)$

**Proof**

1. Deriving the means

$$E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \cdot P(X=x)$$

Substitute the probability mass function (PMF) of the binomial distribution, $P(X=x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$:

$$E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$$

Expand the binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}$:$$E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$$

Since the first term of the sum (when $x=0$) is $0$, we can start the summation at $x=1$:$$E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^{n} x \frac{n!}{x(x-1)!(n-x)!} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$$

Cancel the $x$ in the numerator and denominator:

$$E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^{n} \frac{n!}{(x-1)!(n-x)!} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$$

To reshape this into another binomial expansion, factor out one $n$ and one $p$:

$$E[X] = np \sum_{x=1}^{n} \frac{(n-1)!}{(x-1)!(n-x)!} p^{x-1} (1-p)^{n-x}$$

Now, use a change of variables. Let $y = x - 1$.As $x$ goes from $1$ to $n$, $y$ goes from $0$ to $n-1$.The exponent $(n-x)$ becomes $(n - 1 - y)$.Substitute $y$ into the summation:

$$E[X] = np \sum_{y=0}^{n-1} \frac{(n-1)!}{y!(n-1-y)!} p^y (1-p)^{n-1-y}$$

Rewrite the fractional term as a binomial coefficient:$$E[X] = np \sum_{y=0}^{n-1} \binom{n-1}{y} p^y (1-p)^{n-1-y}$$

According to the Binomial Theorem, $\sum_{k=0}^{m} \binom{m}{k} a^k b^{m-k} = (a+b)^m$. 

Applying this to our sum where $m = n-1$, $a = p$, and $b = 1-p$:

$$E[X] = np (p + (1-p))^{n-1}$$

Geometric Distribution

Let \(X\) equal the number of trials until the first success is obtained in a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials. The event \(X = x\) corresponds to \(x – 1\) failures, and then a success. Thus,

\[P(X=x) = (1-p)^{x-1}p\]

for \(x=1, 2, 3, \dots\)

Derivation of Geometric Distribution CDF

The cumulative distribution function (CDF), \(F(x)\), represents the probability that the first success occurs on or before the \(x^\text{th}\) trial. It is derived by summing the probability mass function from \(k = 1\) to \(x\):

\[F(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{x} (1 - p)^{k-1}p\]

Factor out the constant probability of success, \(p\):

\[F(x) = p \sum_{k=1}^{x} (1 - p)^{k-1}\]

The summation is a finite geometric series with a common ratio of \((1 - p)\). Using the standard formula for the sum of a finite geometric series, \(\sum_{k=1}^{x} r^{k-1} = \frac{1 - r^x}{1 - r}\) where \(r = (1-p)\):

\[F(x) = p \left( \frac{1 - (1 - p)^x}{1 - (1 - p)} \right)\]

Simplify the denominator, noting that \(1 - (1 - p) = p\):

\[F(x) = \frac{p(1 - (1 - p)^x)}{p}\]

Cancel the \(p\) in the numerator and denominator to arrive at the final CDF:

\[F(x) = 1 - (1 - p)^x\]

Properties of Geometric Distribution

  • Mean: \(\mu = \frac{1}{p}\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{q}{p^2}\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \frac{pe^t}{1 - qe^t}\)

**Solution:**

Using the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) for the geometric distribution, the probability of getting the first success on or before the $n^\text{th}$ trial is $P(X \le n) = 1 - (1 - p)^n$.

Set up the inequality with the target probability of 0.80 and $p = 0.10$:
$$1 - (1 - 0.10)^n > 0.80$$
$$1 - (0.90)^n > 0.80$$

Negative Binomial Distribution

  • In the Negative Binomial distribution, we want to find the number of trials needed to achieve the \(r^{\text{th}}\) success.

  • Let \(X\) be the random variable representing the total number of trials required until the \(r^{\text{th}}\) success is obtained.

  • To understand how the probability mass function (PMF) is constructed, we break the sequence of trials into two distinct parts:

Part 1: The first \(x-1\) trials

For the \(r^{\text{th}}\) success to occur exactly on the \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial, there must be exactly \(r-1\) successes occurring somewhere within the first \(x-1\) trials.

Because the order of those earlier successes and failures doesn’t matter, the number of ways to arrange them is given by the binomial coefficient \(\binom{x-1}{r-1}\). Using standard binomial probability logic, the probability of having exactly \(r-1\) successes and \(x-r\) failures by time \(x-1\) is:

\[ \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^{r-1} q^{(x-1)-(r-1)} = \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^{r-1} q^{x-r} \]

(Where \(p\) is the constant probability of success, and \(q = 1-p\) is the probability of failure).

Part 2: The \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial

Since the \(x^{\text{th}}\) trial must be the final success that stops the sequence, its probability is simply \(p\). Using the multiplicative rule for independent events, we multiply the probability from Part 1 by \(p\) to get the final PMF of \(X=x\):

\[ p \cdot \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^{r-1} q^{x-r} = \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^r q^{x-r} \]

Let $X$ be the random variable with the PMF

$$P(X = x) = \binom{x - 1}{r - 1} p^r (1 - p)^{x-r}$$

where $x = r, r + 1,$


Then it is a probability distribution called as negative binomial distribution with parameter $r$ and $p$.

Remarks, if $ r=1$, then the negative binomial distribution was just basically identical to geometric distribution.

Summary of Parameters:

  • \(x\): Total number of trials (\(x = r, r+1, r+2, \dots\))
  • \(r\): Target number of successes
  • \(p\): Probability of success on any given independent trial
  • \(q\): Probability of failure (\(1 - p\))

Note: The Geometric distribution is a special case of the Negative Binomial distribution where we are looking for the very first success (\(r=1\)).

Properties

  • PMF: \(P(X=x) = \binom{x-1}{r-1} p^r (1-p)^{x-r}\) for \(x=r, r+1, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = \frac{r}{p}\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \frac{rq}{p^2}\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \left( \frac{pe^t}{1 - qe^t} \right)^r\)

**Case 1: A win four game**

Since A winning those four game, we P(success)=P (A wins)=0.6. This can be a probability of Negative binomial with parameter r=4 and p=0.6 when $X=6$.

Hence

\begin{align*}
P(\text{A win series in 6}) &= NegBin(6,4,0.6) \\
&= \binom{6-1}{4-1} (0.6)^4(0.4)^{6-4} \\
&= 0.20736
\end{align*}

**Case 2: B win Four game**
Since $B$ winning those four game, 

$$P(success)=P(B wins)=0.4$$

This can be a probability of Negative binomial with parameter $r=4$ and p=0.4 when X=6.

Hence

\begin{align*}
P(\text{B win series in 6}) &= NegBin(6,4,0.4) \\
&= \binom{6-1}{4-1} (0.4)^4(0.6)^{6-4} \\
&= 0.09216
\end{align*}

We wanted to find the probability the series end in $6$ game. So it can be either A win series in $6$ game or B. since the event is mutually exclusive, than

\begin{align*}

Hypergeometric Distribution

Used for sampling \(n\) individuals without replacement from a finite population of size \(N\) containing \(M\) successes.

\(P(X=x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x}\binom{N-M}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}}\) for \(\max(0, n - N + M) \le x \le \min(n, M)\)

The assumptions leading to the hypergeometric distribution are as follows:

  1. The population or set to be sampled consists of \(N\) individuals, objects, or elements (a finite population).

  2. Each individual can be characterized as a success (\(S\)) or a failure (\(F\)), and there are \(M\) successes in the population.

  3. A sample of \(n\) individuals is selected without replacement in such a way that each subset of size \(n\) is equally likely to be chosen.

By definition; let n be the number of samples drawn from the population without replacement and let random variable X be the number of green ball drawn. Its pmf is given by the hypergeometric distribution

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x}\binom{N-M}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}} \]

for \(x\), an integer, satisfying max \((0, n - N + M) \le x \le \text{min } (n, M)\).

Properties

If \(X\) is a random variable with hypergeometric distribution with parameter \(N\), \(M\) and \(n\). then

  1. \(\text{Mean} = n \cdot \frac{M}{N}\)

  2. \(\text{Variance} = \left(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\right) \cdot n \cdot \frac{M}{n} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{M}{N}\right)\)

A lot consisting of $50$ bulbs is inspected by taking at random $10$ bulbs and testing them.If the number of defective bulbs is at most $1$, the lot is accepted; otherwise it is rejected. If there are in fact, $10$ defective bulbs in the lot, what is the probability that the lot would be accepted?

**Solution:**
We use the hypergeometric distribution because we are sampling without replacement from a finite population.

-   $N = 50$ (total bulbs)

-   $M = 10$ (total defective bulbs in the population)

-   $n = 10$ (number of bulbs sampled)

-   $x$ = number of defective bulbs in the sample

The lot is accepted if the number of defective bulbs is at most 1 ($x \le 1$). So we need to find $P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)$.

\begin{align*}
P(X \le 1) &= P(X=0) + P(X=1) \\
&= \frac{\binom{10}{0}\binom{50-10}{10-0}}{\binom{50}{10}} + \frac{\binom{10}{1}\binom{50-10}{10-1}}{\binom{50}{10}} \\
&= \frac{\binom{10}{0}\binom{40}{10}}{\binom{50}{10}} + \frac{\binom{10}{1}\binom{40}{9}}{\binom{50}{10}} \\
&\approx 0.0825 + 0.2667 \\
&\approx 0.3492
\end{align*}

A classification model flags a small batch of $N = 20$ user accounts for suspicious activity, of which exactly $M = 5$ are actually compromised (true fraud). A data analyst randomly selects a sample of $n = 4$ accounts to review without replacement.

1. What is the probability that exactly $x = 2$ of the reviewed accounts are compromised (PMF)?

2. What is the expected number (Mean) of compromised accounts in the sample?

3. What is the variance of the number of compromised accounts in the sample?

4. What is the probability that at most 2 of the reviewed accounts are compromised (CDF)?

**Solution**

1. Using the Hypergeometric probability mass function:

$$P(X = x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x}\binom{N-M}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}}$$$$P(X = 2) = \frac{\binom{5}{2}\binom{15}{2}}{\binom{20}{4}} = \frac{10 \times 105}{4845} = \frac{1050}{4845} \approx 0.2167$$


2. The mean of a hypergeometric distribution is $E[X] = n \cdot \frac{M}{N}$:

$$E[X] = 4 \cdot \frac{5}{20} = 4 \cdot 0.25 = 1.0$$

The analyst should expect to find 1 compromised account on average.

3. The variance of a hypergeometric distribution accounts for the finite population correction factor $\left(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\right)$:

$$Var(X) = n \cdot \frac{M}{N} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{M}{N}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\right)$$

$$Var(X) = 4 \cdot \left(\frac{5}{20}\right) \cdot \left(1 - \frac{5}{20}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{20-4}{20-1}\right)$$

$$Var(X) = 4 \cdot (0.25) \cdot (0.75) \cdot \left(\frac{16}{19}\right) = 1 \cdot 0.75 \cdot 0.8421 \approx 0.6316$$


4. The cumulative distribution function evaluates $P(X \le 2) = P(X=0) + P(X=1) + P(X=2)$.

    - $P(X = 0) = \frac{\binom{5}{0}\binom{15}{4}}{4845} = \frac{1 \times 1365}{4845} \approx 0.2817$
    
    - $P(X = 1) = \frac{\binom{5}{1}\binom{15}{3}}{4845} = \frac{5 \times 455}{4845} = \frac{2275}{4845} \approx 0.4696$
    
    - $P(X = 2) = \frac{1050}{4845} \approx 0.2167$ (from above)
    
    Summing these probabilities:$$P(X \le 2) = \frac{1365 + 2275 + 1050}{4845} = \frac{4690}{4845} \approx 0.9680$$

Poisson Distribution

Let the number of occurrences of some event in a given continuous interval be counted. Then we have an approximate Poisson process with parameter \(\lambda > 0\) if the following conditions are satisfied:

  1. The numbers of occurrences in nonoverlapping subintervals are independent.

  2. The probability of exactly one occurrence in a sufficiently short subinterval of length \(h\) is approximately \(\lambda h\).

  3. The probability of two or more occurrences in a sufficiently short subinterval is essentially zero.

Suppose that we wanted to find the probability of number of occurrence, \(x\) of event in a time period given the length of the period and the average events per time. than the pmf of X is

\[P(x \text{ events in time period}) = e^{\left(\frac{-\text{events}}{\text{time}}\right)(\text{time period})} * \frac{\left(\left(\frac{\text{events}}{\text{time}}\right)(\text{time period})\right)^x}{x!}\]

Usually, in describing poisson distribution, we mainly concerning on the rate of occurrence which events/time * time period is usually simplified into a single parameter, \(\lambda\), lambda, the rate parameter.

Hence, Deff: a random variable X is said to have poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda > 0\) if its pmf is given by

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} \] Where \(x = 0,1,2,3, \ldots\)

To prove that the Poisson distribution is a valid probability distribution, we need to show that

\[\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} = 1\]

Proof From maclaurin series, it is given that

\[e^\lambda = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!}\]

So we can rewrite the pmf of X to be \[ \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} = e^{-\lambda} \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!} = e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda} = 1 \]

Hence it is proven.

If \(X\) is a random variable of poisson distribution wth parameter \(\lambda>0\), then

  1. Mean \(= \lambda\)

  2. Variance \(= \lambda\)

  3. \(M_X(t) = e^{\lambda(e^t - 1)}\)

Describes the number of occurrences of an event in a given continuous interval with an average rate \(\lambda > 0\).

  • PMF: \(P(X=x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}\) for \(x = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\mu = \lambda\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = \lambda\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}\)
